A graph of quantum factors is a quantum causal model only when the factors that share a Hilbert
leg commute pairwise. The library checks this at the freeze boundary, where the graph turns from
dynamic to frozen, and a failure aborts the freeze, names the pair and leaves the graph dynamic.

The example builds one two-node graph twice. Both factors sit on leg 0, so the check has a pair
to test.

## What you see

```text
[1] sigma_z and diag(3, -1) on leg 0
    expected: they commute
    froze cleanly
      pairs tested       1
      worst margin       0.000e0
      is_frozen()        true
...

[2] sigma_x and sigma_z on leg 0
    expected: they anticommute
    freeze aborted
      offending pair     nodes 0 and 1
      reason             ‖[ρ_0, ρ_1]‖_F exceeds the Q-TOL threshold (margin > 1)
      is_frozen()        false
...
```

The margin is the commutator's norm divided by the threshold, so anything at or below 1 accepts and anything above 1 rejects and names the pair.
Two diagonal matrices commute exactly. The first margin is zero, not merely small. The second
pair anticommutes, and its commutator sits as far from zero as a single-qubit commutator gets.

## Why the boundary is the right place

At the freeze the structure is complete and nothing depends on it yet. A check there never
rejects a half-built graph, and never finds the problem after a result has been used. A failure
costs the caller a rebuild. It never costs a result computed on a structure that does not hold.

The error carries `QuantumErrorEnum::CommutatorNonZero { node_j, node_k, detail }`, so the caller
receives the node indices as data.

## Read before you cite

The example passes `None` for the faithfulness argument, so it runs the commutativity check
alone. Supplying the declared inputs and outputs adds the C₃ check over the frozen graph. The
check is sound, since it never accepts a non-commuting model, and it may be incomplete.
