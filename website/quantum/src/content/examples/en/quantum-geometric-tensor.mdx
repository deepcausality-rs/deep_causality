The quantum metric alone gives the flat band a conductivity, 0.037778 meV·nm² in the example, and
band curvature contributes zero. A flat band has no dispersion, so conventional band theory says
it cannot conduct, yet magic-angle twisted bilayer graphene has flat bands and conducts. The
missing term is geometric.

The tensor `Q_ij` gives one complex number per pair of axes. Its real part is the quantum metric,
which measures how far apart neighbouring states are. Its imaginary part is the Berry curvature,
a magnetic field in momentum space.

## What you see

```text
Q_ij for the flat band, and its decomposition
  pair      Re(Q_ij)      Im(Q_ij)      g_ij          Omega_ij
  Q_xx       0.004198      0.000000      0.004198      0.000000
  Q_xy       0.000000     -0.004198      0.000000      0.008395
  Q_yx       0.000000      0.004198      0.000000     -0.008395
  Q_yy       0.004198      0.000000      0.004198      0.000000

The symmetry the decomposition forces
  g_xy - g_yx                 0.000e0   the metric is symmetric
  Omega_xy + Omega_yx         0.000e0   the curvature is antisymmetric
  Omega_xx                    0.000e0   so the diagonal of it vanishes

  D = (D_conv + g~_xx * E_gap) * a^2
    flat band alone             0.000000 meV*nm^2
    with the geometry           0.037778 meV*nm^2
```

The excerpt drops the header and unit lines and keeps each printed row.

## The step that is easy to skip

`Q_ij` comes out in nm², because the velocity matrix elements carry energy times length. The
transport kernel expects the metric divided by the cell area, `g̃_xx = g_xx / a²`. Passing `g_xx`
directly would hand the kernel an area where it expects a ratio, and since the kernel multiplies
the weight by a² again, the answer would be off by the cell area twice over.
